Since a few years I have heard a new mantra in the mouth of multiple managers: let's appoint external consultants to improve the performance of the team or the company. This is also something I have discussed with many people over the last years, and this article is mostly based from the discussions that I have had.
I want to go through a few scenarios that I have witnessed in the last few years in this article.

# Introducing the new consultant

Everything starts with the way the new consultant is presented to the team. I have heard sentences from a manager that sounded like: "I hired a new consultant that is starting next monday, that person is excellent, and will upskill your team and show you how to deliver properly, increase the quality of your work, and increase the teams's velocity".

That kind of message is disastrous.

Most of the time, productivity issues have been discussed in team retrospectives. The team knows what the underlying issues are. But instead of listening to the team, that manager decided to find a different root cause: the team has not been capable of fixing it's own issues. 

Some managers will percieve that some teams have a low productivity. But compared to what? This happens a lot when managers change from one company to another: if they were thriving in a high velocity green field startup, and move to a company with more legacy systems, they will think performance and agility is low. It maybe is, but the team members probably have something to say about that. The system and the constraints are different.

# Snowflakes

For some consultants, it feels it's a disgrace for them to work with other team members. So they let everyone know about how they feel about it.

And why would they come to work in that low-performing environment at all? Because they have negociated some extra conditions.

So some special conditions are written in the contract between the hiring company and the consultant: more remote work (or even full remote), whereas team members get quibbled about they work presence at the office.

The consultant's daily rate is set high, and this information is repeatedely told to collaborators. Of course, the snowflake is expensive, because they are an expert that has more knowledge than existing collaborators. On the other hand, collaborators got no or low raises over the last years (remember, the current team performance is bad, so this explains that).

This creates a form of jealousy from the get go, and a lot of frustration for current team members.

Repeat this multiple times, and managers wonder why internal staff are demotivated and lack engagement.

# The god

Sometimes, the new consultant can't be put inside the team due to the way the team is organised. Sometimes the current teams manager is also considered a low performer, so the new consultant will be put somewhere else in the hierarchy. The consultat might get a shiny title like "staff engineer" or "delivery manager". Sometimes that person is introduced to the teams as having more decision power than the team. Sometimes their role is never really defined and they float through the organisation without any clear boundaries. I have seen people that were even not on the organigram but had more decision power than multiple teams. I have seen consultats which job title changed depending on the mood. One day there were technical expert, the second day CTO, the third day simple senior developer.

Most consultants won't try to fix this situation. The power this gives them is huge. I have seen staff engineers gatekeep a teams work, practically slowing them down, instead of being an accelerator, as it would be expected from a staff engineer.

# The god like team

When you have one problem, why not multiply it. Let's say you have multiple underperformign teams: if you have enough budget, you can probably justify adding a few more experts to your company. Maybe form a team of super-consultants. I call it the god like team.

I have seen these teams: "Delivery teams", "Staff engineer team". Sometimes they are hidden inside a platform team, because it's a natural place to put them, as the platform team has a huge transversal impact. Or maybe create a separate team to work on a project, to show how well they will delivery it (on time, with high quality, and so on).

# Initial impact

Sometimes, the initial impact of the newcomer looks great. They bring a fresh vision, maybe a new set of slides. They often do some sort of audit or 
report of astonishment to the top management. I have seen slides basically throwing an existing team under the bus. Not the best way to make friends and gain the teams confidence.

Also, often teams already reported a lot of problems. No one acted on these problems. Action items from retrospectives have not moved since years, to the point that some collaborators even stopped reporting issues. But as the expert consultant is being listened to, his words have more impact.

Maybe the consultant is put on a green field project, or will propose a new green field way of working in the company.
For example, the exisiting team will be kept working on the legacy system, whereas the consultant or the god like team will work on the new shiny technology, to prepare for the future. I have seen teams struggle for months on keeping a monolith alive, while the expensive consultants could have fun setting up a new microservice architecture.

Also, consultants will not respect any architecture patterns, or propose some quick implementations to get things shipped, completely ignoring the maintenability of the functionnality they are delivering. Of course no one introduced them to the processes that other teams have to follow. How could they, as they are not linked to any sane organisational structure. They are above or next to the current structure. 

The initial delivery velocity will look impressive. It's mechanical, as this is a green field project. Everything can be done without having to take care of the legacy context. After a few months or years, depending on the size of the team and project, everything will slow down. Stakeholders might get upset, as their new shiny toy has not been delivered on time. The initial estimate was bloated: of course it was, this allowed the god like team of consultants to start the project, and secure one full year of budget to pay their salaries.

The god like team often does not care about legacy systems. I have even seen a contract once, where the consultat would never have to work on the legacy system. I have seen contracts where sconsultants would only do "build", and could not be used for incident or bug management ("run"). Basically "I build it, someone else runs it".

Once stakeholders start complaining, or once the project has been finally delivered: it's time for the consultant to leave and wreak havock somewhere else, leaving the company with a half-backed solution. The existing team will inherit that solution, and will often get pressured into finishing the work. This is also not really promising.

# The ghost god

Another variation is when the consultant is full remote, and does not deliver much. Depending on their initial aura, they might last a few months in the company, until a few people start asking questions, and then that consultant just magically gets removed from the project. Odd. But this will surely leave an even worse taste in the resident collaborators.

# Gods and agility

Remember the agile manifesto? It says: The best architectures, requirements, and designs emerge from self-organizing teams.

But to the god team, that's not true. Teams are unable to self-organize. Proof, this is why they have been hired: to fix broken teams. And then starts a fascinating anti-agile pattern: the god consultant or the god like team will start meddling in all the minute details of the team, trying to change things from top down. They will often start doing a lot of hand-holding and micro-management, to try to shape the team to their vision.

I have witnessed teams change from a well working Kanban to Scrum, just because the consultant put so much pressure on that team to change this part of the process, which was not even broken.
Then, the time of the daily meeting got changed, because "it had to be in the morning"; even though part of the team was sitting in another time zone and had to sacrifice their lunch break to make the daily meeting happen "in the morning" for the other half of the team.
Finally, the consultants started participating in ticket redaction and intervened in the daily meeting to reframe discussions.
How on earth is the team supposed to self-organise in this case.

# Solutions

I did not want to finish this blog post without any recommendations.

Of course, you can, and you should hire consultants and experts. Of course, some teams are pretty junior and will need a little bit of help. But there is a subtle way to help them.

First, and foremost, consult the team to check if they need help. Make them verbalise their issues. Retrospectives are there for that. Or one to one's with the different team members. Ask them. You would be surprised what they have to say.

During the hiring process, involve one or two team members. Let the tech lead for example sit in the hiring meetings. Let them make the final call on which candidate is hired. Make your employees part of that process to gain support.

During the contracting phase: create no excemptions. No full remote if the team is not full remote. No special accomodations about the projects they will work on. Any small excemption will be hard to explain to the team.

Then, once the expert is hired, the onboarding is very important. Make it cristal clear to the consultant, to the team, and even outside of that team's scope, what the role of the consultant is.

Then, as a manager, keep a thight control of the consultants activities. Get feedback from team members as fast as possible. Make sure the intial changes proposed by a consultant are reviewed and validated by the team. Make sure the boundaries are clear and are not crossed.
Make sure that not everything get's changed in a short period of time: this is an immediate red flag for me. A team has a capacity to change that is sometimes limited: change needs time and repetition.

Invert the dynamic for green field projects: put current team on it. Hire consultants to maintain the legacy system. Your internal team should have the fun, and also, they are building their own future. This will make sure your team members will keep engaged and motivated.

# Clarifications

I have nothing against consultants. I consider them a necessary part of the tech ecosystem. I have met really inspiring experts, but they are rather the exception in my experience. Or they have been ill-advised by their management, asked to change things that probably should not have been changed.

As always, opinions are mine.
