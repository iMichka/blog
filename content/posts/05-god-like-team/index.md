Companies hire consultants because they want to solve problems they can't solve themselves.

But what happens when the consultant isn't hired to help the team solve the problem, but to solve the team?

I've seen this happen several times: a consultant arrives with a shiny title, a large daily rate, special working conditions and a mandate to “improve” a team that has already been explaining its problems for years.

At that point, the consultant stops being an expert brought in to help. They become something else.

A god.

And when you hire multiple gods, you might even create a divine council in your company.

The problem isn't hiring consultants. The problem is using consultants as a replacement for listening to, trusting, and empowering your existing teams.

## The consultant arrives

Everything starts with the way the new consultant is introduced to the team.

I have heard sentences from a manager that sounded like:

"I hired a new consultant that is starting next Monday, that person is excellent, they will up-skill your team and show you how to deliver properly, increase the quality of your work, and increase the teams's velocity".

That kind of message is disastrous.

Most of the time, productivity issues have been discussed in team retrospectives. The team knows what the underlying issues are. But instead of listening to the team, that manager decided to find a different root cause: the team has not been capable of fixing it's own issues.

Some managers will perceive that some teams have a low productivity. But compared to what? This happens a lot when managers change from one company to another: if they were thriving in a high velocity green field startup, and move to a company with more legacy systems, they will think performance and agility is low. It maybe is, but the team members probably have something to say about that. The system and the constraints are different.

## The consultant becomes special

Why would a really good consultant, expert of his field, want to work in a low-performing environment at all?

Because they have negotiated some extra conditions.

For example more remote work (or even full remote), whereas team members get quibbled about they work presence at the office.

Or the consultant's daily rate is set high, and this information is repeatedly told to collaborators. On the other hand, collaborators got no or low raises over the last years (remember, the current team performance is bad, so this explains that).

This creates a form of jealousy from the get go, and a lot of frustration for current team members.

## The consultant gets authority

Sometimes, the new consultant can't be put inside the team due to the way the team is organized. Sometimes the current teams manager is also considered a low performer, so the new consultant will be put somewhere else in the hierarchy. The consultant might get a shiny title like "staff engineer" or "delivery manager".

Sometimes that person is introduced to the teams as having more decision power than the team.

Sometimes their role is never really defined and they float through the organization without any clear boundaries.

I have seen people that were even not on the organigram but had more decision power than multiple teams. I have seen consultants which job title changed depending on the mood. One day there were technical expert, the second day CTO, the third day a senior developer.

I have been in a meeting where two different people claimed to be the CTO of the company at the same time. This was the most hilarious moment of my life.

Most consultants won't try to fix this situation. The power this gives them is huge.

Sometimes, the initial impact of the newcomer looks great. They bring a fresh vision, maybe a new set of slides. They often do some sort of audit or report of astonishment to the top management.

I have seen slides of a report of astonishment throwing an existing team under the bus. Not the best way to make friends and gain the team's confidence.

Also, often teams already reported a lot of problems. No one acted on these problems. Action items from retrospectives have not moved since years, to the point that some collaborators even stopped reporting issues. But as the expert consultant is being listened to, his words have more impact.

## The divine council

When you have one problem, why not multiply it. Let's say you have multiple underperforming teams: if you have enough budget, you can probably justify adding a few more experts to your company. Maybe form a team of super-consultants.

I call it the divine council.

I have seen these teams: "Delivery teams", "Staff engineer team". Sometimes they are hidden inside a platform team, because it's a natural place to put them, as the platform team has a huge transversal impact. Or maybe create a separate team to work on a project, to show how well they will delivery it (on time, with high quality, and so on).

## The consultant builds something shiny

Maybe the consultant is put on a green field project, or will propose a new green field way of working in the company.
For example, the existing team will be kept working on the legacy system, whereas the consultant or the god like team will work on the new shiny technology, to prepare for the future. I have seen teams struggle for months on keeping a monolith alive, while the expensive consultants could have fun setting up a new micro-service architecture.

Also, consultants will not respect any architecture patterns, or propose some quick implementations to get things shipped, completely ignoring the maintainability of the functionality they are delivering. Of course no one introduced them to the processes that other teams have to follow. How could they, as they are not linked to any sane organizational structure. They are above or next to the current structure.

The initial delivery velocity will look impressive. It's mechanical, as this is a green field project. Everything can be done without having to take care of the legacy context. After a few months or years, depending on the size of the team and project, everything will slow down. Stakeholders might get upset, as their new shiny toy has not been delivered on time. The initial estimate was bloated: of course it was, this allowed the god like team of consultants to start the project, and secure one full year of budget to pay their salaries.

I have even seen a contract once, where the consultant would never have to work on the legacy system.

I have seen contracts where consultants would only do "build", and could not be used for incident or bug management ("run"). Basically "I build it, someone else runs it".

Once stakeholders start complaining, or once the project has been finally delivered: it's time for the consultant to leave and wreak havoc somewhere else, leaving the company with a half-backed solution. The existing team will inherit that solution, and will often get pressured into finishing the work. This is also not really promising.

## Gods and agility

Remember the agile manifesto? It says: The best architectures, requirements, and designs emerge from self-organizing teams.

But to the god team, that's not true. Teams are unable to self-organize. Proof, this is why they have been hired: to fix broken teams. And then starts a fascinating anti-agile pattern: the god consultant or the god like team will start meddling in all the minute details of the team, trying to change things from top down. They will often start doing a lot of hand-holding and micro-management, to try to shape the team to their vision.

I have witnessed teams change from a well working Kanban to Scrum, just because the consultant put so much pressure on that team to change this part of the process, which was not even broken.
Then, the time of the daily meeting got changed, because "it had to be in the morning"; even though part of the team was sitting in another time zone and had to sacrifice their lunch break to make the daily meeting happen "in the morning" for the other half of the team.
Finally, the consultants started participating in ticket redaction and intervened in the daily meeting to reframe discussions.
How on earth is the team supposed to self-organize in this case.

## Solutions

I did not want to finish this blog post without any recommendations.

Of course, you can, and you should hire consultants and experts. Of course, some teams are pretty junior and will need a little bit of help. But there is a subtle way to help them.

First, and foremost, consult the team to check if they need help. Make them verbalize their issues. Retrospectives are there for that. Or one to one's with the different team members. Ask them. You would be surprised what they have to say.

During the hiring process, involve one or two team members. Let the tech lead for example sit in the hiring meetings. Let them make the final call on which candidate is hired. Make your employees part of that process to gain support.

During the contracting phase: create no exceptions. No full remote if the team is not full remote. No special accommodations about the projects they will work on. Any small exception will be hard to explain to the team.

Then, once the expert is hired, the onboarding is very important. Make it crystal clear to the consultant, to the team, and even outside of that team's scope, what the role of the consultant is.

Then, as a manager, keep a tight control of the consultants activities. Get feedback from team members as fast as possible. Make sure the initial changes proposed by a consultant are reviewed and validated by the team. Make sure the boundaries are clear and are not crossed.
Make sure that not everything get's changed in a short period of time: this is an immediate red flag for me. A team has a capacity to change that is sometimes limited: change needs time and repetition.

Invert the dynamic for green field projects: put current team on it. Hire consultants to maintain the legacy system. Your internal team should have the fun, and also, they are building their own future. This will make sure your team members will keep engaged and motivated.
